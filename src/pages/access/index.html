Access

What a model is allowed to touch has shaped AI as we use it more than the weights or the benchmarks have. Here is that whole surface, drawn as the one Windows app that was already a tree of what a machine can reach.

Sixty-eight nodes. Each one is a flavor of access, not a product, so a node outlives the vendor that currently occupies it. Products hang off the nodes as examples, and every node that isn't finished carries a dated bet.

Five states, all of them Device Manager's own. Installed works and something depends on it. A yellow bang works badly enough to matter. An unknown device is present on the bus with no driver, which is where somebody is actively building. Disabled is the one worth reading twice: in Device Manager a red X never meant missing, it meant switched off, which is exactly what an access looks like after the ecosystem passed it over. And a device can be not present at all, describable but attached to nothing, which is where the speculation lives. Those are hidden until you ask for them, under View.

Dates are month-level where the month is establishable and year-level where it isn't, marked with a ~. I would rather show you a coarse date than a confident wrong one.

Read it the way you'd read a tech tree in a game, because the branches behave like one. Some are sequential: you cannot take the file system before code execution, and the Properties sheet on any device shows its full prerequisite chain. Some are zero-sum, drawn as amber double-ended lines, where investment in one branch is exactly what stops the other being maintained. Some are unexplored, the phantoms nobody has attached. And some are ignored, already built and switched off. Every device previews what it buys and what it costs on hover, because a pick with no downside is a pick nobody had to think about.

    Why access, and not capability

    Model generalization is worse than it looks from the outside. Pattern matching across that many dimensions builds correlations out of bad priors and coincidence, and correlation still isn't causation. So you make a model right about a specific situation by handing it that situation. Better priors are the slow road to the same place.

    That reframes every node on this board. Access substitutes for the generalization the model doesn't have, which is why one log line beats a day of careful reasoning about what the bug probably is, and why Feedback is the most underbuilt branch here.

    The access that would make the rest of this board smaller

    Every node here exists because the model keeps nothing that happens to it. The log, the failing test, the page, the production trace: all of that is access standing in for a lesson the weights never hold on to. Close that loop and a good deal of this board becomes optional, which makes weight-level learning the one entry worth arguing against the whole premise with. It gets two nodes, because two different things are being asked for and only one of them is hard.

    Editing its own weights is the temporary version: fit the parameters to the task in front of you, use them, discard them. It works. An 8B model doing this scored 53.0% on the ARC public validation set in Nov 2024, roughly six times its fine-tuned baseline, and 61.9% ensembled with program synthesis, which was the average human score. Google has built the same idea into two architectures since, Titans in Jan 2025 and Nested Learning's HOPE on 7 Nov 2025. None of it is something an agent can reach for, because it is an architecture choice a lab makes before anyone sees the model, and that is the unknown-device state exactly.

    Weights that keep the lesson is the durable version, and the surprise is that it shipped at an editor company rather than a lab. Cursor's Tab model has trained on live accept and reject signal since 12 Sep 2025, across 400 million requests a day, rolling a fresh checkpoint every 1.5 to 2 hours. The same loop reached Composer on 26 Mar 2026 on a five-hour cycle. The measured gains are small and real: 21% fewer suggestions at a 28% higher accept rate on Tab, and on Composer 2.28% more agent edits surviving in the codebase against 3.13% fewer dissatisfied follow-ups.

    Read what that loop returns, though, because it closes at the population. Your rejected completion improves the model everyone gets five hours later and teaches your agent nothing about your codebase, so the thing every session starts without is still the thing nobody ships. That is why it carries a yellow bang instead of sitting in the installed column, and why Autobiographical continuity is still a phantom beside it. The industry's answer to a model that cannot keep a lesson has been to put the lesson in the context window and pay for it again every session, which is why Personal memory is the node it competes with rather than the node it depends on.

    The loop stays open for an ordinary reason, and gradient descent is not it. A frozen checkpoint is what makes an eval mean something, a safety case hold, and a rollback exist, and weights that move in deployment have none of the three. So continual learning sits in the same category as Release authority: withheld on purpose, by people who could ship it, which puts it on the trust axis rather than the research one.

    Trust is the gating dependency

    The vertical axis is blast radius, from reading what it's handed to moving atoms. Every rung up buys capability and spends trust, and that's why the top of the board is thin: the blockers up there are authorization, not engineering. Secrets, identity, and money are one dependency chain, and Cloudflare wiring agentic commerce onto the same Web Bot Auth it uses to sort crawlers is that chain showing up as a roadmap.

    The gaps are the interesting part

    Fifteen devices carry a yellow bang, twelve have no driver, ten are switched off, and thirteen are not present at all. A few are genuinely hard. Most are unbuilt because the thing they'd need is a standard, an index, or somebody's permission, and no single vendor can ship any of the three alone.

    The two states people skip are the ones I'd look at first, and together they are 23 of the 68 devices here. That weighting is deliberate. The installed column is where the conference talks are; the other two are where the information is.

    Switched off: ten answers nobody wanted

    Disabled means the ecosystem built the thing and then declined to use it. Ten nodes are in that state and the dates are the point: 1993, 1999, 2001, 2002, 2004, 2005, 2011, 2011, 2014, 2024. Every current complaint about agent access has a corpse behind it.

    Agents drive desktop apps by looking at screenshots, and those apps published dictionaries of their own verbs in 1993. Tool discovery has no index, and WSDL plus UDDI shipped one in 2001. Agent-to-agent messaging is being specified now, and XMPP standardised federated addressing and capability discovery in 2004. Nobody subscribes to anything, and Atom was an RFC in 2005. Sites are asked to declare their AI preferences, and P3P did machine-readable policy in 2002 and was formally obsoleted.

    Relitigating any of them buys nothing. The useful move is to notice that every one failed the same way: it needed a consumer with leverage and got a standards document instead. That is a much better predictor of which current proposal survives than how good the proposal is, and it is why the newest nodes on this board carry my least confident bets.

    Not present: where the speculation lives

    Not present is an access precise enough to describe with nothing attached to it, which is the only honest place to guess. Thirteen nodes, hidden until you ask for them under View, and they fail for three different reasons worth telling apart.

    Some are blocked on permission, not capability. Ambient sensing needs no research at all; the hardware and models exist and the people in the room never agreed. Organizational memory means reading things people said while assuming nobody would keep them. These do not get unblocked by engineering, and treating them as engineering problems is how you build something nobody is allowed to ship.

    Some are blocked on nobody being paid to build them. The discard pile needs a team to store its failures; simulation needs consequence-free rehearsal that robotics has funded for a decade and software has not. These are the ones I would actually go build, because the obstacle is attention rather than physics.

    And one is withheld on purpose and should stay that way. Release authority is the gap between a passing test and a running service, and almost every team draws that line deliberately. This site is a worked example: its CI token cannot write, no deploy path may create infrastructure, and only a machine-owned branch can publish, specifically so the automation cannot reach production. That node sits empty because the map is recording a decision, which is the whole reason to have a "not present" state instead of a "missing" one.

    So every unfinished node carries a bet, with a date and a confidence I'm willing to be wrong about in public. High confidence means I'd argue it now. Low means it's a shape I can see and a timeline I can't. The bets are the speculative part of this page and they're labelled everywhere they appear, because a map that quietly mixes forecast into fact is worse than no map.

    Under most of these nodes there's a standard nobody mentions

    The product layer gets the announcements and the standards layer decides what's actually possible, usually two years earlier and in a draft with a revision number. Three of the nodes here are standards work rather than products: a vocabulary for crawl preferences at the IETF, in-page tool declaration in a W3C community group, and the accessibility tree, which shipped in 2014 for entirely different reasons and became the thing computer-use agents lean on.

    Reading the drafts is worth it, because the failure mode is legible in them well before it's legible in a product. AIPREF is the clean example: the vocabulary half is moving along the Proposed Standard track, and the half that says how to attach it to a page lapsed under the six-month rule and hasn't been revised. That asymmetry tells you the fight is over who has to honour it, never over what to express. And WebMCP's API moving off navigator onto the document mid-origin-trial is the ordinary sound of a spec still deciding what it is.

    The pattern worth taking from this: the web platform keeps building the machine-readable surface first and the consumer never arrives. That is why Declared surfaces is its own branch here, and why most of that column is switched off. Seven of its ten nodes are red X, every one of them predates the agent era, and the oldest is from 1999. Each was built for a consumer that turned out not to want it, which is a bad bet on timing more than a bad build. Agents are the first plausible consumer any of them has ever had.

    Two of them are worth reading against their live descendants, because the board puts them side by side on purpose.

    Self-describing APIs is the sharpest, because that one got tried. Plugins read OpenAPI directly in 2023 and were retired inside a year, and MCP took the slot by shipping a protocol and a client instead of a description. Service contracts and registries is the same lesson twenty-five years earlier: WSDL plus UDDI is precisely the schema-and-index pair that Tool discovery carries a yellow bang for lacking today. The generalisation is uncomfortable. A machine-readable artifact with no runtime attached loses to a worse artifact that arrives with one, and the registry is never the hard part.

    Machine-readable policy is the one that should make you cautious about the newest node on this board. P3P let a site state, in a form a machine could act on, what may be done with what it collects. It became a W3C Recommendation in 2002, one browser implemented it, sites published claims nobody checked, and it was formally obsoleted. Stated crawl preference is now attempting the same thing for AI training, and the difference cannot be expressiveness, because P3P was expressive. It has to be a consumer with leverage, which means a CDN or a court rather than a working group.

    MCP is the counterexample that proves the rule, and it is worth holding up beside the corpses. It shipped a client before it shipped a committee: a protocol with a runtime attached in Nov 2024, ten thousand public servers, and only then, in Dec 2025, a donation to a Linux Foundation body whose co-founders are the two labs competing hardest with the one that wrote it. Every dead node in this column did that in the other order.

    What a year of vendor release notes actually added

    The dates on this board used to come from announcements, which is a biased sample: an announcement is a thing somebody wanted you to notice. So the pass behind this revision read the boring surface instead, entry by entry, across every lab that keeps a public log. Anthropic's weekly Claude Code digest, OpenAI's API changelog and its Codex notes, Google's Gemini API release notes, and for the open-weight labs the model cards, which is where Moonshot, DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral and Z.ai make their agent claims because none of them ships a platform changelog to make them in.

    Three findings, and none of them is a model.

    The 2026 additions cluster on three nodes, and all three make an existing access cheaper rather than granting a new one. Permission stopped being a click and became a classifier. The context window stopped being a wall and became a budget the agent rewrites. Speed stopped being a property of the model and became a line item you buy at 2x the price for 2.5x the rate. Two of those were not on this board before this pass and are now, which is the honest cost of having drawn it from announcements the first time.

    Feedback got almost nothing, again. Across roughly a year of entries, the branch this page already called the most underbuilt picked up an eval product that is being switched off, two dashboards that watch the vendor's agents rather than yours, and one genuinely good tool that streams a running process into the conversation. That is it. The one closed feedback loop of the period turned up outside the sample entirely, at an editor company reading its own traffic rather than in a lab's changelog, and it is on the board now as Weights that keep the lesson. I would rather have found the argument wrong; a year of concentrated vendor attention going almost everywhere else is stronger evidence for it than the argument was.

    And the platform layer is churning faster than the access layer under it. In one quarter: Meta shut its Llama API on 6 Jul 2026, Google retired Gemini CLI on 18 Jun 2026, and OpenAI removes the Assistants API, its first agent-shaped product, on 26 Aug 2026, alongside its visual agent builder and its eval platform on 30 Nov. Not one flavor of access on this board went with them. That is the premise of the whole page getting demonstrated three times in ninety days, and it is the reason the nodes are named for what they reach rather than for who currently sells it.

    the one path that isn't a dependency

    Prompt injection is neither a node on this board nor an edge. Turn on Trace the injection path and the flow routes through the context window instead of the graph, because that's where untrusted tokens and privileged actions actually meet. Every arrow in is a page an agent fetched. Every arrow out is something it can do. Nothing in the dependency structure separates them, which is the whole problem, and it's why Secrets is the node where the good answer turned out to be never letting the agent read the credential at all.
