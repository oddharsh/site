AV2, before anyone can see it

AV2 is the successor to AV1, the codec inside every AVIF image, and its reference encoder shipped 1.0.0 in May. AOM's own all-intra report puts it 18 to 24% ahead of AV1 on stills. That number comes from video test sequences scored with PSNR, SSIM and VMAF. I wanted the answer for photographs, scored the way this site already scores its own images, at the exact byte budgets it ships. No browser can decode an AV2 image yet, so the figures below are decodes I made offline, and one section brings its own decoder to draw AV2 in your browser anyway.

These are three copies of the same 128 px crop. The first is AV1, the codec inside the AVIF files browsers decode today. The second is AV2 in the same container, at 2,030 bytes against AV1's 2,097. The third is how you'd try to ship AV2 safely: a <picture> source typed image/avif, with a WebP fallback.

In Chrome 154 and Canary 156 the third image is broken, and the WebP never loads. Of everything on this page, that's the finding most likely to cost somebody an afternoon, so it comes first.

Decoding it here instead

Your browser couldn't draw that second file, but this page can. The button below fetches AOM's reference decoder, AVM 1.0.0, compiled to WebAssembly, and runs it on a background thread. It's about 550 KB over the wire, which is why it waits for a click. It draws exactly the pixels the native build does: I compared every decoded sample on 16 files, from 8-bit monochrome to 10-bit 4:4:4, and all of them matched.

The number under each one is its bits per pixel. Each AV1 image is the AVIF file the site actually serves, drawn by your browser's own decoder. Each AV2 image is the largest AVM 1.0.0 encode at or under that file's size, at speed 6 with TCQ, the IST/CCTX search and per-segment QM on (see below), cut from the same tile and drawn by the WebAssembly decoder.

Flip swaps the two in one frame on a click or Space, so your eye stays put. Side by side puts them in two frames that pan together; drag either one. The source tile, before either codec touched it, sits behind its own hold button (or hold S), so a quick back and forth never passes through it.

On these 11, AV2 leads on 5 colour tiles between 0.12 and 1.17 bits per pixel and trails on 3 of the 4 above 0.8, which is the curve the table further down describes. The 3 Monochrom tiles come out level: within 0.2 points of ssimulacra2 each, at 0.27 to 0.47 bits per pixel, where colour tiles of the same density all went to AV2. Three photos from one camera can't say why, and I haven't traced it.

Every conforming AV1 decoder produces the same samples, and this AV2 decoder matches AVM's own, so what changes when you flip is the encode. Each sample is a photo whose shipped AVIF I could rebuild byte for byte from its original, which proves both files started from the same pixels. Of the 165 originals on disk with a shipped tile, 111 rebuilt; the other 54 are left out. A HIF's 10 bits are reduced to the site's 8-bit tile before either codec sees it.

Why the fallback doesn't catch it

A <picture> element chooses a source by its declared type, before any byte decodes. Chrome supports image/avif, so it commits to the source, fetches it, and finds an image it can't read. Nothing sends it back to the <img>, because the fallback only covers types a browser has never heard of.

The file explains the rest. libavif writes AV2 inside the same box structure as AV1, and the brand still says avif, mif1 and miaf. Only the image item changes, from av01 to av02, with an av2C config box beside it. A careful decoder like avifdec skips the item it doesn't know and reports Primary item not found. A browser draws a broken image.

So an AV2 image needs a name a browser can decline, the way image/avif gave AV1 stills a name that browsers without AV1 could skip. Until one exists, serving AV2 under the AVIF name breaks the image in every browser that supports AVIF, which is every current release of Chrome, Firefox, Safari and Edge.

Updated 2026-10-16. Two things moved. Measured on the tier itself, with 40 tiles cut from HIF originals and picked evenly across it by shipped bits per pixel, AV2 at speed 6 came out about even with AVIF: +0.14 ssimulacra2 weighted to the tier, about 135 wins of the 255 tiles it had then. Then I found speed 6 had been switching off two of AV2's own additions over AV1. The thumbnails are 38 tiles at 600 px, cut the way the site's own pipeline cuts them: the whole frame, squared and reduced about 8x in linear light by zenc square. They come from Fuji photos that none of the tuning below ever saw. Each tile's budget is the file size of the AVIF tier this site ships (avifenc -q 63 -d 10 --speed 2 --yuv 444), and AV2 is encoded onto that budget, file against file. Both are scored against the source with the two perceptual metrics this site ships by: ssimulacra2, where higher is better, and butteraugli, where lower is better.

Matched bytes is the whole method, and AV2 makes it harder than usual. libavif's quality scale is far too coarse for it: on one crop, -q 49 wrote 6,865 bytes of AV2 and -q 50 wrote 9,608, a 40% step. So the probe steps AV2's own quantizer instead, one notch at a time.

These rows let AV2's encoder choose its own superblock size, and at 320 px it picks 64. Pinning the 128 that a real 600 px tile gets moves speed 6 to -1.57 on train and -2.12 on holdout.

What decides it: bits per pixel

The tiles and the crops sit at different densities. At the AVIF tier's budget these 38 tiles average 0.46 bits per pixel and a crop 1.08, about 2.3 times as many, because a crop is all detail and a tile is mostly sky, skin and shadow at thumbnail scale. Pool the two, sort by bits per pixel, and AV2's standing follows one curve. The last column counts this site's 255 shipped 600 px tiles in each band.

A pixel-peeping crop lives at the thick end, which is where the next section finds the loss.

Where AV2 loses

At the dense end, the loss has a shape. My first guess was that AV2 smooths away sensor grain, and I nearly published it. It's wrong. Measured on the decodes, AV2 reproduces the source's fine, pixel-scale texture more faithfully than AV1 on 21 of the 32 calls. The correlation between each decode's fine detail and the source's rises from 0.773 to 0.811.

What AV2 gets wrong is the coarse picture. Average each image over 8 by 8 pixel blocks, which is roughly what you'd see from across the room, and compare those averages with the source's. AV2's error is higher than AV1's on 32 of 32 calls, by 50% in brightness (0.26 to 0.39 on an 8-bit scale) and 64% in colour (0.25 to 0.41). It holds on the 8 calls where the two files sit within 1% of the same size, so it isn't a byte shortfall.

So this encoder moves its bits from the broad strokes to the fine ones: slightly truer texture, slightly wronger tone and colour in flat areas. ssimulacra2 scores the picture at several scales and weighs colour heavily, and it reads that trade as a loss.

Each figure shows the 200 px region where the AV1 and AV2 decodes differ most, at 2x, in lossless WebP, because re-encoding a decode would add artifacts of its own. Click or tap a figure to flip between the three in place, which is the easiest way to see a codec difference. Each AV2 file is the largest that fits at or under the AV1 file, so AV2 never gets the extra bytes.

The tune you didn't choose

butteraugli's verdict turned out to depend on a setting nobody asked for. libavif tells AV2's encoder to tune for SSIM unless you pick a tune yourself, and AV2's own command-line encoder defaults to PSNR. At speed 6, switching libavif to tune=psnr costs AV2 about half a point of ssimulacra2 and doubles its butteraugli wins, from 7 of 32 to 14.

AV2's encoder accepts those two tunes and refuses a third: tune=iq, the image-quality tune libaom added for stills in 3.13, which the shipped AVIF uses. So the comparison pits a codec with a tune built for photographs against one that has only the video defaults. That's a fair picture of today, and a reason to expect the gap to narrow.

Where the literature's number comes from

AOM's report measures something real and different. Its AV2 beats an AV1 anchor by 17.82% on PSNR-Y and 23.58% on VMAF in all-intra mode, which codes every frame as a still. The anchor is a branch of libaom, the test material is video sequences, and the metrics are PSNR-HVS, PSNR, SSIM, MS-SSIM, CIEDE2000 and VMAF. Neither ssimulacra2 nor butteraugli appears, and I couldn't find the encoder speeds in it.

It also reports cost: on average AV2 took 33 times longer to encode than the anchor and 3.86 times longer to decode. The encode column above agrees in kind. Through libavif, one 320 px thumbnail takes 5.4 seconds at speed 6 and 18.3 at speed 3, against 0.45 for the AVIF this site ships. AV2's raw encoder at cpu-used 1 was still running after 8 minutes when I stopped it.

So the two results don't contradict each other. By the metrics video is judged by, AV2 is the better codec. On photographs, its first encoder at its default speed only ties the AVIF this site ships. With two of its own tools turned back on, it wins on most thumbnails by a photo metric, and it still gives ground as the detail rises. AV1 froze its bitstream in June 2018, and Chrome shipped AVIF in August 2020, after faster encoders had arrived. I expect AV2 to need the same run-up before the dense end turns too.

Lossless

AV2's lossless mode round-trips all 16 crops bit for bit. It's also 29% larger than AV1's, which surprised me, because AOM lists improved lossless coding among AV2's new tools.

JPEG XL's lead is the older, bigger story, and nothing here changes it.

What the reference encoder leaves on the table

AVM is a reference encoder, and a reference encoder leaves tuning to whoever comes next. I ported four ideas from the encoders that tune AV1 for stills, and one that only AV2 can express. Each is a patch that is off by default, and the untouched reference decoder reads its output pixel for pixel. Each was scored on the crops, where AV2 needs the help most.

tool-unlockturns back on what the speed level or libavif switches off, one tool at a time+0.99 with TCQ and IST/CCTX (held-out tiles, tier-weighted)wins

Only the last one pays, and it is the one AV1 had no syntax for. AV1 signals one quantization-matrix level per plane for the whole frame. AV2 can carry up to four sets and let each segment pick one. The all-intra curve helps smooth crops and costs 2 to 3.5 points on busy ones, so one frame-wide matrix is the wrong granularity. Splitting the frame with jpegli's masking field, a flatter matrix for textured areas and the curve for smooth ones, turns the curve's 0.47 loss into a 0.14 gain on the holdout crops, winning 11 of 16. On the tiles it adds 0.12 over plain AV2 and wins 28 of 38.

The biggest gain needed no porting at all. AVM's speed levels are tuned for video, and at speed 6 they skip TCQ and the IST and CCTX transforms. The last patch only turns them back on. It was scored on the tiles: together they add about a point of ssimulacra2 across the tier, at about 2.75 times the encode time.

The same build compiles AVM's own AVX2 kernels for Apple silicon through SIMDe. AVM ships almost no NEON code, so on a Mac most of its hot loops ran in plain C. The output is byte-identical to the plain build, and a 600 px encode runs 1.35 times as fast.

Run it yourself

Everything above comes from committed tools. The AV2 builds live in av2-tone, which this site pins by commit. Its encoder/build.sh builds libavif at a pinned commit with its experimental AV2 codec switched on. No tagged libavif release carries AVM 1.0.0 yet, so that pin is a commit too. --tuned adds the six patches and the SIMDe kernels, and refuses to finish unless the result matches the pristine build byte for byte with every option off. Here, av2-tile-probe.ts runs the tile comparison and codec-knob-probe.ts --codec avm runs the crops. av2-tone's wasm/build.sh compiles the decoder this page runs, and samples.ts writes the four AV2 thumbnails, refusing any photo whose shipped AVIF it can't reproduce byte for byte.

bun run av2:build
bun tools/photos/av2-tile-probe.ts --src <a folder of originals>
bun tools/photos/codec-knob-probe.ts --codec avm --variants base,tunepsnr
bun run av2:wasm

With every scheduling and caching concern stripped out, the probe is four steps per crop. Encode the shipped AVIF and take its size as the budget. Find the two AV2 quantizer settings whose files straddle that size. Decode both and score them against the source. Interpolate the score to the exact budget. The rest of the file is caching, crop selection and parallelism, which only change how long it takes.

What would change this

The result is small, and it cuts both ways. It covers 38 tiles, sparser than the tier they sample, and 16 crops from one camera. It runs the reference encoder rather than a production one, in a container libavif itself labels experimental, and scores with two metrics that both come from the JPEG XL project. A production AV2 encoder is the likeliest thing to move the dense end, the way SVT-AV1 and later libaom releases did for AV1, and a still-image tune is the first place to look. The thin end is already a win, and it covers a third of this site's thumbnails. To ship that third, AV2 waits on a browser that decodes it and a name that browser can decline. When either arrives, both probes re-run in an afternoon.
